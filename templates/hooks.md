# Hooks and titles

The first three seconds decide everything. The hook is two things at
once: a line of text on screen (rendered as a PNG, see
`scripts/make_hook.py`) and the first line of narration. They should
say the same thing in different words — the text is the shock, the
voice is the story starting.

## Rules

1. **The hook is a fact, not a question.** "They were told to lick
   the paintbrush" beats "Did you know workers licked paintbrushes?"
   Questions let the viewer answer "no" and swipe. Facts create a gap
   the viewer has to close.
2. **One named person, one impossible situation.** "A 22-year-old
   woman sued the company that was killing her" — person, stakes,
   conflict, all in the first line.
3. **Promise something later.** The hook must contain a reason to stay
   past the first scene: "Stay with me, because five dying women took
   them to court." Without the promise, the viewer has seen the whole
   video in three seconds.
4. **Never open on a static frame.** The first frame needs movement —
   the zoom is already running at t=0 — and something must change
   within 3 seconds: a cut, a caption pop-in, a camera move.
5. **The hook text is short.** Two lines, five words a line at most.
   If it doesn't fit on a phone screen at a glance, it's not a hook.

## Patterns that worked

These are from real videos on the channel, with what made each one work.

**The order that sounds insane**
> "They were told to lick the paintbrush — every single day."
> (Radium Girls, 1917)
> Why: an instruction nobody would follow, stated flatly. The viewer
> needs to know who gave the order and what happened next.

**The number that can't be right**
> "He survived the fall from 10,000 feet — and walked out of the jungle."
> (Juliane Koepcke, 1971)
> Why: the number does the work. No adjectives needed.

**The decision with the world on it**
> "On September 26, 1983, one man decided whether the world ended."
> (Stanislav Petrov — see examples/petrov-1983/)
> Why: a date, a person, the highest possible stakes, and the answer
> withheld.

**The thing everyone got wrong**
> "The company had warnings — and said nothing."
> Why: betrayal is a stronger hook than disaster. Disaster happens;
> betrayal has someone to blame.

## Patterns that didn't

- **"The untold story of..."** — "untold" is what every faceless
  channel says. It tells the viewer nothing about this video.
- **Starting with a date and a place and nothing else** — "It was 1858
  in London" is a textbook opening. The date belongs in scene 2, after
  the hook has already landed.
- **A hook that gives away the ending** — "How five women won their
  court case" leaves nothing to wait for. The hook promises the fight,
  not the verdict.

## Title templates

Titles live on YouTube, not in the video, but they follow the same
rules: named person, stakes, ending withheld. Keep them under 60
characters where possible.

- `She [did the impossible thing] — Then [the twist]`
- `The [ordinary person] Who [extraordinary outcome]`
- `[Year]: The [number] [people] Who [verb] [the powerful thing]`
- `He Was Told to [order]. He [did the opposite].`

What to avoid in titles: "shocking", "unbelievable", "you won't
believe", "mind-blowing". They read as spam to viewers and to the
algorithm both, and they promise a feeling the video then has to
deliver on instead of a story.

## The ending

Every Short ends the same way: the payoff lands, then a question to
the viewer ("Would you have fought back?"), then the spoken and
on-screen reminder — like, comment, subscribe. The question matters
more than the reminder: a comment is worth more than a like, and a
question is what gets one.
