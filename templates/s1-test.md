# The S1 test — does this topic deserve a video?

Every topic gets scored before a single clip is generated. Four checks,
all four must pass. A topic that fails one of them gets dropped, not
fixed — a weak topic with good editing is still a weak video.

## The four checks

### 1. A named person or animal

The viewer needs someone to follow. "A factory in 1917" is a place.
"Grace Fryer, 22, painted watch dials in 1917" is a person. Animals
work the same way: Hachiko the dog, the emu army of 1932.

Fail: a topic about a system, a trend, or a year with no one in it.

### 2. Extreme stakes

Something has to be on the line that a stranger cares about in three
seconds. Death, prison, ruin, a fortune, a war starting — not
"interesting" or "surprising". "Interesting" is not a stake.

Fail: "the surprising history of ketchup" — unless someone died over
it (someone did, in the ketchup-as-medicine story, which is why that
one passed).

### 3. The payoff is held to the end

The viewer must not know how it ends until the last scene. If the
outcome is obvious from the hook, there is nothing to wait for. The
hook promises an ending; the middle earns it; S6 delivers it.

Fail: a story whose ending is in the title, or a story with no ending
at all — just a list of facts.

### 4. US resonance

The audience is American. The story should touch something they
already know or feel: their history, their wars, their cities, their
fears, their nostalgia. A story can be set anywhere — the Radium Girls
were in New Jersey, Onoda was in the Philippines — but the emotional
hook has to land in the US.

Fail: a story that needs three minutes of context before it means
anything to someone in Ohio.

## The 65+ note

The channel's biggest audience turned out to be viewers over 65. That
changed the topic list: WWII, the Cold War, the space race, 1950s–80s
America, and nostalgia-era stories outperform everything else. The S1
test stays the same, but when two topics both pass, the one an older
American viewer lived through wins.
